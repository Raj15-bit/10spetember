A fuel price is now setting the world's interest rates

A closed strait has pushed diesel into producer prices and producer prices into rate expectations, and central banks on 3 continents are tightening together. In India a record foreign inflow has left the banking system full of cash and the currency weaker anyway, while the country's manufacturing headlines keep measuring the last step of a chain whose hard parts are still bought in.

01

How a fuel price became a rate decision

Diesel, not crude, is the line that runs from a closed strait into a central bank meeting.

The price of a barrel of oil stopped being a commodity story and turned into an interest-rate story. Saudi Arabia told OPEC that its crude production has fallen to 6.2 million barrels a day, the lowest since 1990, a drop of 1.9 million barrels a day. Tanker-tracking data puts its exports down about 40%, from 5.1 million barrels a day to 3.1 million. Brent crude went through $100, then $103, then $105, then $108.

The part that reaches everybody else is not crude but diesel. Diesel is the fuel that trucks, trains, ships, tractors and factory generators run on, so its price sits inside the cost of almost every delivered good. American diesel rose 24.1% in 1 month and set a record at $5.94 a gallon, up 72% over 9 months. That single fuel accounted for over a third of the monthly rise in the index that measures what American producers charge for finished goods.

Producer price inflation then came in at 5.4% against the 5.3% economists expected, with the core measure at 4.6% and the previous month revised up. Within hours the argument moved from when rates would be cut to whether they were about to rise: 62% odds of an increase at the next Federal Reserve meeting and 71% by October, under a chairman, Kevin Warsh, who has not yet made a single rate decision. The 10-year US government bond yield reached 4.93%, a 3-year high, which means the price of borrowing dollars went up for everyone who borrows in them, including India.

02

Both exits are contested

The plan for moving oil without the strait now runs past a port that changed hands.

The Strait of Hormuz is a channel about 3 kilometres wide at its narrowest and it is the only sea exit from the Gulf. With it effectively shut, Saudi Arabia turned to a second route, pipelines running west to the Red Sea. That plan has now met its own obstacle. The Houthis have taken the Yemeni port of Mocha, which gives them more control over Bab al-Mandab, the strait at the far end of that same Red Sea route. Both exits from Arabian oil are now contested, so the alternative is not really an alternative.

The cushion behind it is thin. The American emergency oil reserve stands at 285 million barrels, about 15 million above its lowest level ever. A weekly crude draw came in at 391,000 barrels when 1.35 million was expected, while stocks of distillates — diesel and heating oil — built by 2.087 million barrels. In India, Indian Oil refineries are running at 105% of rated capacity, so there is no spare processing left to bring on.

The second physical limit is electricity, and it binds on a longer clock. Nvidia is working with Firmus, CDC, NEXTDC and AirTrunk to reach up to 2 gigawatts of Australian data centre capacity by 2027, when the country's entire existing computing base is about 1.6 gigawatts. In Telangana, Fortune Hospitality plans a ₹60,000-crore data centre park on 170 acres that could reach ₹1 lakh crore and 1,200 to 1,600 megawatts across 4 modules of 400 megawatts. The state transmission utility has allocated 2,000 MVA, but only 400 MVA arrives by December 2027 and the full supply not until December 2034. The buildings take about 3 years and the power takes 8.

03

The whole world is tightening at once

4 central banks moved or signalled in the same direction, and the bond market went first.

The European Central Bank raised its deposit rate by 25 basis points to 2.50%, its second increase of the year, and lifted its inflation forecast for the next 2 years to 2.1% by 2028. Christine Lagarde declined to commit to a path, but officials expect more tightening with October in play and traders put a third increase this year at 90%. The German 10-year yield rose 6 basis points to 3.51%, the highest since 2009, which tells you the bond market had already decided.

In Japan the same direction is showing up more slowly. Board member Kazuyuki Masu said underlying inflation is now very close to the 2% target and warned the Bank of Japan may have to raise rates quickly if it accelerates. The Bank still holds about 46% of all Japanese government bonds, down 8 percentage points from its 2023 peak but more than banks, life insurers, pension funds and foreign investors combined; it owned roughly 10% in 2013. Its holdings fell $310 billion over 12 months, the largest such decline on record, to about $3.3 trillion. The 10-year Japanese yield rose 5 basis points to 2.93%.

American demand for long bonds is intact; the price is not. A 30-year auction cleared at 5.308% with a stop-through of 2.7 basis points, the second largest in a decade, and the second highest foreign demand ever recorded — buyers turned up, but only once the yield was high enough. The Treasury capped its buyback at $6 billion, below what the market wanted. Against that, President Trump has promised a $5,000 payment to every adult citizen if his party holds Congress: about 245 million people and roughly $1.2 trillion, on top of a deficit already running near 7% of output with unemployment at 4% and inflation above 2% for 60 consecutive months.

04

Who pays, and who collects

The barrel gets the headline; the refined fuel is where the money actually moves.

Crude gets the headline and refined fuel moves the money. The gap between what a refinery pays for a barrel and what it sells the diesel for — the crack spread — widened towards $100 a barrel, which is where refiners collect and hauliers pay. Petrol matters to households; diesel matters to the industrial economy, and the producer price data showed which of the 2 is doing the damage.

Gold fell on the hot inflation print, on the ordinary logic that higher inflation brings higher interest rates and higher rates make holding a metal that pays nothing less attractive. There is a serious argument against that reading. When a government's debt is large enough that interest costs themselves drive the budget, raising rates makes the debt problem worse rather than better, and gold is priced against exactly that problem. The last time the United States was in that position it capped bond yields and let the currency take the strain. Total US debt is now above $40 trillion.

2 industrial metals moved for reasons that had nothing to do with demand. Copper shares fell after it emerged the White House has not decided on refined copper tariffs, weighing higher input costs for manufacturers against encouraging domestic mining. A large American tool distributor warned that the tungsten cost shock is still hitting factory floors — tungsten is what cutting tools are made from, so it raises the cost of making almost anything by machine. OPEC output as a group fell by 900,000 barrels a day.

05

The money came in and the rupee still went out

A record foreign inflow, a banking system awash with cash, and a currency that ignored both.

India raised an extraordinary amount of foreign money and the currency still went the wrong way. Through the Reserve Bank of India's concessional swap window, inflows reached $136.4 billion, with banks alone gathering $127.2 billion of foreign currency deposits against market estimates of $90 billion to $100 billion. The response was strong enough that the window was closed a month early. System liquidity — spare cash sitting in the banking system — rose to nearly ₹10.5 trillion.

Money that large has to be put somewhere. Axis Bank chief executive Amitabh Chaudhry said plainly that it could lead to abnormal lending, because banks will have to deploy it, and the 3 obvious homes are 5-year government bonds, replacing costlier deposits, and corporate loans where credit growth is already strong. Meanwhile the rupee weakened past ₹95 to the dollar, foreign investors were net sellers in the cash market for a second running day with short positions at 89%, government bond yields climbed, and India is now the World Bank's largest borrower.

The repo rate is 5.25% and the stance is neutral. Forecasts now point to 50 basis points of increases split between October and December, and the reason is not that the economy is overheating. Consumer inflation was about 2% last year and is expected near 5% in the coming year, so the governor has said the rate may need recalibrating from 5.25% simply because the starting point was unusually low. A rate rise here would be catching up with the price level, not leaning against growth. The monsoon was the weakest in over a decade, which pushes food prices the same way.

06

The export number counts the last step

India assembles the phone, the board, the magnet and the missile — and imports the hard part of each.

India exported smartphones worth ₹2.6 lakh crore in the last financial year, and that number counts the last step of the chain. Inside a phone is a circuit board no thicker than a credit card, holding 8 copper layers, plated holes through every layer, holes too fine for a drill so a laser makes them, and a bendable section on plastic film. Making one is 2 jobs: fabricating the bare board, then soldering several hundred components onto it. Dixon, Syrma SGS and Kaynes do the second at scale; the first still happens in Taiwan, China and Korea.

That is beginning to change, and the word to notice in the policy paperwork is first. Under the electronics component scheme, 75 applications carrying ₹61,671 crore of investment and 65,040 jobs have been approved. The newest tranche of 29 contains India's first flexible circuit board plant, its first surface-mount passive plant and its first rare-earth permanent magnet facility, the last to Lohum Cleantech. Kaynes is building boards in Chennai up to 74 layers, in a country importing close to 90% of its high-density boards.

The same shape appears elsewhere. Rare-earth magnet imports fell to $163 million from a peak of $291 million, which reads like substitution and is not — China restricted exports, so the fall measures a supply cut. In defence, approvals worth ₹1.1 trillion were cleared with about 98% earmarked for domestic manufacture, and exports rose 151% — yet chips, sensors, laser weapons and special steels are still bought in whole.

Restricting a raw material also forces engineers to redesign around it. Tesla has put a permanent-magnet motor using no rare earths on the road in its Cybercab, 3 and a half years after setting the goal, the same pattern as American chip controls pushing Chinese labs to squeeze more from weaker hardware. The restriction moves the engineering rather than stopping it, so waiting to be granted access is the weakest option open to India.

A phone that costs more than a salary

The premium end is compounding while more than half the two-wheeler market runs on petrol.

The clearest single comparison in Indian consumption right now is a price against a salary. Apple's folding phone launched in India at ₹2,99,900 for the smallest storage and ₹4,49,900 for the largest. An Infosys fresher's package is ₹2,50,000 a year. The phone costs more than the first-year salary of a graduate engineer, and it is selling.

The premium end is compounding. Electronics Mart India upgraded its guidance past 20% revenue growth with gross margins above 15%, reported double-digit growth in big appliances in a seasonally weak quarter for the first time in 4 or 5 years, and now sells Apple directly instead of through Redington. CaratLane grew 42% in its latest quarter with its buyer base up 28%, and plans 40 to 50 new stores on top of more than 388. Shankesh Jewellers lifted revenue to ₹423.58 crore from ₹273.17 crore and its margin to 14.44% from 11.65%, doubling profit.

The mass end looks nothing like that. Petrol two-wheelers were 94% of rural registrations, with electric versions at 5.9%, against 10.7% in cities and 10.9% in the largest metros. Rural India is 55% of all two-wheeler registrations, so more than half the market is barely electrified. TVS at 29% and Bajaj at 27% hold 56% of the rural electric sales that do happen.

Swish, a 10-minute food delivery business, raised $24 million after passing 1 million orders a month, 3 times its volume 6 months earlier, from 50 pin codes in Bengaluru and Delhi. At Swiggy, over 50,000 delivery partners filed tax returns through the partner app claiming ₹6.5 crore of refunds, more than a third of them for the first time. Against all of it, salaried workers across healthcare, technology, finance and blue-collar jobs give the same answer when asked: the work is stressful, the load is rising, the pay does not feel like enough.

The figure quoted and the figure earned

Pipelines, one-month consolidations and reclassified revenue lines, all technically accurate.

The same quarter at Gaja Alternative Asset Management can be written 2 ways using only the company's own figures. Read one way, revenue fell 49% to ₹15.64 crore from ₹30.76 crore, other income came in at ₹36.19 crore, and operating profit was minus ₹2.06 crore against ₹14.15 crore. Read the other way, fee income rose 7% to ₹15.6 crore, performance income rose 17% to ₹29.7 crore, treasury income rose 444% to ₹6.5 crore, total income rose 26% to ₹51.8 crore and profit rose 35% to ₹27.2 crore. Nothing is wrong in either version. What changed is which line is called revenue.

Scale and economics are also being confused. Tega Industries reported consolidated revenue of ₹1,720 crore, but that includes only 1 month of the acquired Molycop. Split apart, the legacy business did ₹430 crore at a 22.1% operating margin while Molycop did ₹1,290 crore at about 13%. Net debt is ₹6,366 crore, provisional goodwill about ₹5,000 crore, and the synergy target is roughly $20 million over 2 to 2.5 years. The company got much bigger; it has not yet got better.

Then there are pipelines standing in for orders. Sathlokhar Synergies quotes an active pipeline of ₹22,654 crore against an unexecuted order book of about ₹1,140 crore, so the pipeline is 20 times what has actually been won. Systematic Industries is associated with a ₹1,000 crore figure that is a tender opportunity rather than an order book. At Hawkins Cookers, profit after tax rose to ₹131 crore while cash from operations fell from ₹174 crore to ₹102 crore to ₹88 crore over 3 years, which is one reason its fixed deposit scheme keeps returning — this one at 8% for 36 months, against 10.5% in 2019.

Rails, transformers and substations

The money in the compute build-out keeps landing on the physical layer, not the model layer.

The gap between benchmark borrowing costs in the United States and China is the widest it has ever been, which changes the direction money wants to travel between the 2 largest economies. Around it, the physical parts of the compute build-out are producing strange league tables. King Slide's founder Lin Tsung-Chi is now Taiwan's richest man at $17.8 billion. His company makes drawer slides and precision rail kits — the metal rails a server sits on. That puts him ahead of Anthropic's Dario Amodei and Foxconn's Terry Gou at $15.5 billion each, TSMC's Morris Chang at $10.2 billion and OpenAI's Sam Altman at $3.3 billion.

The pattern repeats in China. BYD is showing a solid-state transformer power module built by a subsidiary that is a core partner on Nvidia's 800-volt direct-current rack, and it cuts the cost of a rack by 25% to 30%. A carmaker's electrical engineering is being sold into data centres. At home BYD is losing ground: retail share is 15.2% as Geely at 171,000 units and Leapmotor at 85,000 eat into the cheap end, while Tesla exported 36,000 cars out of its Chinese plants rather than selling them there.

Manufacturing capacity is being decided in the same period. South Korea is close to an American energy investment worth more than $100 billion, financing up to 8 nuclear plants and a gas project to power the build-out. TSMC posted record monthly revenue of NT$514.8 billion, about $16.35 billion, up 53.3% on the year. Within India the same clustering shows: Karnataka has about 65% of aerospace exports and 67% of defence aircraft and helicopter manufacture, while Telangana's aerospace and defence exports reached ₹32,850 crore, 33.4% of the state's exports, more than double the ₹15,940 crore of 2 years earlier and now ahead of pharmaceuticals at 25%.

Arguments worth keeping open

5 questions where both sides hold up, and the specific thing that would settle each.

The richest disagreement is about artificial intelligence, and it is no longer about whether the models work. OpenAI has published a resolution of the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem, 1 of the 7 Millennium Prize problems and unsolved since it was posed. Agents reached it about 88 hours after being launched, consuming roughly 130 billion output tokens on that problem alone and sending 2.7 million messages, with the proof checked in Lean, a system that verifies mathematical arguments mechanically. The company says it has made substantial progress on a second such problem. Independent mathematicians have not yet worked through the argument.

The other side is not that the capability is fake, but that capability and effect are different clocks. Electric motors existed by the 1880s and factory productivity did not surge until the 1920s, because the first factories simply swapped a steam engine for 1 big motor and the gains only arrived when buildings were rebuilt around a motor per machine. Meanwhile the concrete bill is already visible: maintenance costs for machine-written code have risen 300% in 18 months, duplicated code is up 48% and refactoring has fallen 60%. Microsoft's finance chief says the constraint is how fast supply can be made revenue-ready, not the models. So the bottleneck is organisational rather than cognitive, and the money made in the meantime keeps landing on rails, transformers and substations rather than on the models themselves.

Every term used above, in 1 sentence each.

BarrelAbout 159 litres of crude oil. Prices are quoted per barrel.
Brent crudeThe oil grade used as the world benchmark price, drawn from North Sea fields.

Bab al-MandabThe strait at the southern end of the Red Sea, the other way out of the region by sea.
OPECThe group of oil-exporting countries that coordinates production levels.
DistillatesThe refined fuels that come off in the middle of the process, mainly diesel and heating oil.

Core inflationInflation with food and fuel stripped out, meant to show the underlying trend.
Basis point1 hundredth of 1 percentage point. 25 basis points is 0.25%.
YieldWhat a lender earns for holding a bond to maturity; it rises when the bond's price falls.
Bond auctionThe sale where a government issues new debt and buyers bid the yield they will accept.
Stop-throughAn auction clearing at a lower yield than the market expected, meaning demand was strong.
BuybackA government purchasing back its own outstanding bonds, usually to support their price.
Deposit rateThe rate a central bank pays commercial banks on money parked with it; the main policy lever in Europe.
Repo rateThe rate at which India's central bank lends to commercial banks, currently 5.25%.
Neutral stanceA central bank saying it is not leaning towards raising or lowering rates.
Fiscal dominanceWhen government debt is large enough that interest costs, not inflation, drive policy choices.
Nominal growthGrowth measured in current prices, before inflation is taken out.
System liquiditySpare cash sitting in the banking system, available to lend or invest.

Foreign portfolio investorAn overseas fund buying listed shares and bonds rather than building a business.
Cash marketBuying and selling shares outright, as opposed to trading contracts on them.
Short positionA bet that a price will fall, taken by selling something you do not own.
OptionsContracts giving the right, not the obligation, to buy or sell at a set price.
Premium turnoverThe money actually paid for options, a better measure of activity than contract counts.
Volatility indexA gauge of how much movement traders expect; a low reading means calm is being priced.
Offer for saleExisting owners selling their shares, so the money goes to them and not to the company.
Grey market priceWhat unlisted shares change hands for privately before they start trading on an exchange.
Index heavyweightA company large enough that its share price moves the whole index.
Smallcap and microcapThe smaller listed companies, ranked below the large and mid-sized ones by market value.

Printed circuit boardThe layered board inside a device that connects all its electronic components.

High-density boardA board using very fine laser-drilled holes to pack more circuitry into less space.
Surface-mount passiveSmall components such as resistors and capacitors soldered directly onto a board's surface.
